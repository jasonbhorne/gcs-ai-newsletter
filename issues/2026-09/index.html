★ Editor's Pick

Venecky instead of Kentucky: parents suspect school agendas are AI-generated

A Louisville middle school handed out student agendas with a mangled US map that renamed Kentucky as Venecky, a scrambled periodic table, and moon phases nobody could read. Students were told to tear out pages 19 through 36. Families had already paid a $10 fee for them. The failure here was not a teacher misusing a tool. It was unreviewed vendor output that nobody checked before it went to a printer and then went home with kids. That is the cheapest lesson in this issue, and it costs nothing to apply.

Analog Inspiration

Two cards to try this month

I had the privilege to attend ETSU's teaching and learning conference a few weeks ago, the Conference for High-Impact Instructional Practices, and the keynote speaker had these really cool "Analog Inspiration" cards. I'm going to be posting two a month for you: one for students; one for teachers. I hope you enjoy these. They are a way to deal with AI while retaining a lot of analog in the process.

From a GCS teacher who wrote her own AI expectations for first graders:

AI is like a helper. It can give us ideas and answer questions, but our brains are the most important part of learning. I will use AI to help me learn. I will think first, ask kind questions, check my answers, keep myself safe, and always do my own best work.

Ellie, first grade, GCS
FrictionTeacher move

Try to beat your own assignment with AI.

Take one assignment you already give and use AI to finish it in a way that skips the learning entirely. Then ask what you would change to add new complexity, or a moment of productive friction, so the thinking still has to happen somewhere.

Start with the assignment you're tired of grading. This is usually the best AI use-case.
Critical ThinkingStudent move

Write first, then ask AI, then compare.

Pose an open-ended question from your lesson and give students a few minutes to write their own answer. Then have them ask AI the same question and compare: what is similar, what is missing, what new questions come up. Pair them up to discuss, then debrief as a class.

Writing first is the trick here. It's a good experiment.
