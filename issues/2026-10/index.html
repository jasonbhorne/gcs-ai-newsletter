✨ This Month in AI & Schools

The backlash got specific

Last month the argument about AI in schools was mostly opinion. This month it has numbers, dates, and contracts attached. New York City shut off generative AI for about 600,000 students through 8th grade, Los Angeles followed within hours, and the teachers unions got Microsoft to sign a student data privacy standard that takes effect November 1. The OECD's PISA results landed the same week, with a 28 point science gap between students who never use AI and those who use it daily, and the head of PISA wrote it up in The Economist under a headline that does not hedge. New this issue: a section for everyone who runs the buildings, buses, kitchens, offices, and network, because this goes to all of us and most of it has been written for classrooms. The Gemini in Classroom change we flagged in September is now getting the national coverage it deserved, and it is still the one setting worth confirming on our own system.

★ Editor's Pick

Teachers unions announce AI safety agreement with Microsoft

This is the rare policy story with a date and an action attached. The AFT negotiated a legally enforceable standard with Microsoft: no training AI models on student or educator data, no selling it, no advertising use, and the district keeps control over retention and deletion. It takes effect November 1, it is open to every district in the country, and it can be added to a new or existing Microsoft contract without renegotiating anything. We run on Microsoft. That makes this a question to ask, not a project to start.

CNN

Nation's largest school district bans AI in the classroom through 8th grade

A one year moratorium on generative AI for roughly 600,000 New York City students, pre-K through 8th grade, and they are disabling the AI components of more than 38 programs that were previously allowed. High school keeps a limited slate of tools plus an AI critical thinking course. The line worth noticing is the teacher carve out: staff can still use AI for lesson planning and operational work, but not for grading or assessment.

Protecting organizations from AI-assisted executive impersonation and invoice fraud

For the business office, and for anyone who approves a payment. Over three days in August, one campaign sent more than a million emails to accounts payable staff, most of them in the US, impersonating each company's CEO and asking for an ACH payment of about $50,000 on a fake invoice. Proceed With Caution

The watch-outs

Not reasons to avoid AI. Reasons to go in with your eyes open.

The Economist

Stop turning schoolchildren into crash-test dummies

The head of PISA, writing the week the 2025 results came out, and the argument is in the title. Across 760,000 fifteen year olds in 91 countries, students who never use AI for schoolwork scored 509 in science. Daily users scored 481. That 28 point gap is about a year and a half of teaching. The exception is the part worth reading twice: students who used AI weekly, and specifically to help them learn, beat everyone, including the non users. His line is that learning is a productive struggle with new material, and AI either scaffolds that struggle or short circuits it. Paywalled, but the OECD's own summary of the findings is free.

Schools Caught Flat-Footed After Google Makes Gemini Chatbot Available to All Students

We flagged this in September and it is still worth confirming. In August, Google opened the Gemini tab in Classroom to K-12 students of all ages instead of 18 and up. The catch that matters: it only appears where a district had already granted students access to Gemini, so if it was off for students it stays off. Compare defaults before you panic. Microsoft Copilot ships off for student accounts, OpenAI's free teacher workspace excludes students, and Anthropic bars minors outright. As one expert puts it, Google defaults to adoption rather than permission.

Analog Inspiration

Two cards to try this month

Two more from the Analog Inspiration deck I picked up at ETSU's Conference for High-Impact Instructional Practices. One teacher move, one student move, every month. Both of these are about slowing down just long enough for the thinking to happen.

CritiqueTeacher move

Let AI do your assignment badly, then let students grade it.

Ask AI for a response to one of your assignments that would pass but not impress. Hand that out with your rubric. Students score it on their own first, then pair up to compare and calibrate, then you debrief as a class. By the end they have argued their way to a shared understanding of what good actually looks like on this assignment, which is a conversation that usually never happens.

Students are harder graders than we are. That is the whole trick.
DiscernmentStudent move

Before you use AI, ask what you are avoiding.

The question is one line: what am I trying to avoid by using AI right now? Confusion? Perfectionism? Boredom? Sometimes the honest answer is that the work is hard, and hard is where the learning was going to happen. Have students jot down what they notice. A thirty second pause is enough to turn reaching for AI into a decision instead of a reflex.

This one works on adults. I tried it on myself and did not love the answer.
